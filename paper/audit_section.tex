\section{A Data-Quality and Leakage Audit of the Public PPD Survey Benchmark}
\label{sec:audit}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:audit:motivation}

The Kaggle ``PostPartum Depression'' survey is the most frequently used public structured
dataset in computational PPD research, and reported accuracies on it routinely approach or
exceed \SI{99}{\percent}. We set out to use it to train and validate the structured/clinical
branch of a multimodal architecture. Auditing it instead produced a result we consider more
useful: the benchmark cannot support the performance claims made on it, and the standard
evaluation protocol applied to it is invalid. We report five findings.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Effective sample size.} The file does not behave like a sample of independent
        respondents. Its 1{,}168 analysable rows contain 248 distinct questionnaire answers,
        and accounting for how unevenly those 248 are repeated (Kish's design effect) puts the
        effective sample size at \textbf{109}.
  \item \textbf{A two-stage deflation.} Reported scores are inflated twice over: once by
        duplicated respondents straddling a random split, and again by scoring rows rather than
        distinct questionnaires. Fixing both drops ROC-AUC from $\approx$0.99 to $\approx$0.90
        to \textbf{$\approx$0.73}.
  \item \textbf{Construct validity.} Under corrections appropriate to the clustering in the
        data, only \textbf{one of nine} candidate predictors survives as a defensible
        correlate of the outcome.
  \item \textbf{An improvement ceiling.} Thirteen models---four families tuned two ways, five
        learners suited to small categorical data, and a pre-specified ensemble, all tuned
        directly on the metric we report---fail to beat a small-grid Random Forest. The
        ceiling is a property of the data, not of modelling effort.
  \item \textbf{Operating point.} For screening use, the decision threshold changes recall far
        more than the choice of classifier does.
\end{enumerate}

We release corrected reference baselines under a leakage- and weighting-controlled protocol,
and recommend that protocol as the default for survey-derived mental-health datasets.

\subsection{Dataset and target}
\label{sec:audit:data}

The dataset comprises 1{,}503 self-administered Google Form responses: an age bracket and nine
categorical symptom items. Following prior work we take \texttt{Suicide attempt} as the target,
a coarse severity proxy rather than a clinical diagnosis. Its \texttt{Not interested to say}
responses ($n=335$) are non-response rather than a third class and are dropped, leaving
\textbf{1{,}168 rows at a \SI{39.3}{\percent} positive rate}.

Preprocessing canonicalises the instrument's inconsistent option wording; notably the appetite
item offers both \texttt{No} and \texttt{Not at all}---the same answer under two labels---which
we merge. Imputation (27 missing cells, \SI{0.18}{\percent}) and scaling are fitted inside
training folds only; a dedicated benchmark (\S\ref{sec:audit:validity}) confirms the imputation
method makes negligible difference at this missingness rate.

\subsection{Finding 1: an effective sample size of 109--248}
\label{sec:audit:n}

The nine items admit 14{,}580 possible response combinations against 1{,}168 rows. Yet the data
contains only \textbf{248 distinct response patterns}: 920 of 1{,}168 rows
(\SI{78.8}{\percent}) duplicate another row, and one pattern recurs 33 times.

Two independent tests reject that this arose from independent sampling. Simulating 1{,}168
respondents drawn independently from the data's own marginals predicts $\approx$999 patterns
occurring exactly once; the file contains \textbf{one}. A second, assumption-free test grants
the observed pattern frequencies entirely and asks only whether the \emph{counts} look like
random sampling from that fixed distribution---it still rejects, predicting 39 singletons
[26--54] against the one observed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_audit_patterns.png}
  \caption{The benchmark does not behave like independent respondents.}
  \label{fig:audit:patterns}
\end{figure}

Treating each response pattern as a survey cluster, Kish's design effect is
\[
  \mathrm{deff} = 1 + \mathrm{CV}^2(\text{cluster sizes}) = 10.68,
\]
giving a Kish effective sample size of $n_{\text{eff}} = 1168 / 10.68 \approx \textbf{109}$.
This is the standard survey-methodology answer to ``how many independent observations does
this data actually contain,'' and it is more conservative than simply counting the 248
distinct patterns, because it also penalises how \emph{unevenly} those patterns are repeated.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_stats_duplication.png}
  \caption{(a) Repeat count by label. (b) The duplication is not label-neutral: patterns
           positive for the outcome are repeated less (mean $3.9\times$) than negative ones
           (mean $5.4\times$), shifting row-level prevalence (\SI{39.3}{\percent}) away from
           pattern-level prevalence (\SI{47.2}{\percent})---a permutation test rejects
           label-independence of the duplication at $p=0.030$. (c) Sample size under three
           accountings.}
  \label{fig:audit:duplication}
\end{figure}

The prevalence shift in panel (b) matters beyond sample size: row-weighted statistics are not
just noisier than pattern-weighted ones, they are systematically biased toward whichever class
happens to be duplicated more.

\subsection{Finding 2: a two-stage deflation, not one}
\label{sec:audit:leakage}

Two separate defects inflate reported scores, and they compound.

\paragraph{Stage 1---duplicate leakage.} A random train/test split places near-identical
questionnaires on both sides, because \SI{78.8}{\percent} of rows duplicate another row. We
compare standard stratified 5-fold CV (\textbf{Protocol A}, what the literature reports)
against \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} grouped on the response pattern (\textbf{Protocol B}), so
identical questionnaires never span a split.

\paragraph{Stage 2---row-weighting.} Grouping stops leakage but not a second problem: the test
fold is still scored one row at a time, so a pattern repeated 33 times is scored 33 times. If
those repeats are replication artefacts rather than 33 real respondents, this silently
reweights the benchmark toward whichever questionnaires happen to be duplicated most. We
additionally score Protocol B \textbf{pattern-weighted}---collapsing each distinct
questionnaire in the test fold to one point before computing AUC.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_audit_roc_pair.png}
  \caption{Protocol A vs Protocol B, row-weighted. Stage 1 alone already costs the flexible
           models most of their apparent separation.}
  \label{fig:audit:roc}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{The three-stage deflation (Random Forest, representative of the pattern across
           models).}
  \label{tab:audit:stages}
  \begin{tabular}{llc}
    \toprule
    Stage & What it fixes & ROC-AUC \\
    \midrule
    Protocol A --- standard CV & (nothing --- literature as reported) & \textbf{0.998} \\
    Protocol B --- grouped CV, row-weighted & leakage & \textbf{0.895--0.901} \\
    Protocol B --- grouped CV, \textbf{pattern-weighted} & leakage + weighting & \textbf{0.703--0.730} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The pattern-weighted figure is corroborated three separate ways: by the grouped-CV evaluation
(RF 0.717 [0.653, 0.778]), by a from-scratch learning-curve experiment that trains on a growing
pool of distinct questionnaires and evaluates on entirely unseen ones (plateauing at 0.66 AUC),
and by the nested, nothing-held-back tuning pass in \S\ref{sec:audit:ceiling}
(RF 0.730 [0.673, 0.795]). All three land in the same 0.66--0.73 band.

The inflation from Stage~1 alone still scales with model capacity: logistic regression loses
0.06 AUC moving from Protocol~A to Protocol~B, while the RBF-SVM's F1 collapses from 0.989 to
0.464 (4 false negatives become 318). Capacity to memorise is an advantage exactly when the
test set contains rows already seen; Stage~2 removes a further, distinct source of optimism
that survives even after Stage~1 is fixed.

\subsection{Finding 3: construct validity---one predictor out of nine}
\label{sec:audit:validity}

Each of the nine items was tested for association with the outcome under six tests of
increasing rigor: a naive $\chi^2$ on rows; a Rao--Scott design-corrected $\chi^2$; a
cluster-permutation test; a $\chi^2$ computed on distinct patterns only; and two versions of a
multivariable logistic model (cluster-robust, and fit on distinct patterns), each Holm- or
BH-adjusted across the nine items.

Naively, 7 of 9 items look significant. Correcting only for clustering (Rao--Scott) already
drops that to 5. Requiring the association to hold on distinct patterns alone---no design
correction, just no duplicate weight---drops it to \textbf{1: Irritable towards baby \&
partner}. Every other item's apparent significance is, at least in part, an artefact of the
same duplication structure documented in \S\ref{sec:audit:n}.

This corroborates and sharpens an earlier non-monotonicity observation: four items have their
\emph{lowest}-risk level in the middle of the response scale rather than at ``no symptom,'' and
a composite severity score built by summing the eight symptom items---a natural EPDS-style
construct---has \textbf{AUC 0.491 on distinct patterns}, indistinguishable from chance, with
Spearman $\rho=-0.016$ ($p=0.81$) between the score and the outcome. Scale-reliability
diagnostics confirm the items do not cohere: Cronbach's $\alpha=\mathbf{-0.22}$ on distinct
patterns (negative---worse than no scale at all), $\omega=0.03$, and $\mathrm{KMO}=0.51$
(barely admissible for factor analysis).

\subsection{Finding 4: an improvement ceiling that thirteen models cannot cross}
\label{sec:audit:ceiling}

A natural objection to Findings 1--3 is that the $\approx$0.70--0.73 AUC band might reflect
under-tuned baselines rather than a property of the data. We tested this directly.

\paragraph{Design.} Thirteen models, sharing identical outer splits
(\texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold}, 5 folds $\times$ 3 repeats) so every comparison is paired:
the four original baselines, reproduced exactly (small hand-picked grids, tuned on
row-weighted AUC---what a typical paper would report); the same four families
\textbf{improved} (wide randomised search of 10--20 configurations, regularisation-heavy
spaces, tuned directly on \textbf{pattern-weighted AUC} via an inner-fold scorer that groups
validation rows on their questionnaire, needing no group labels); five learners suited to small
categorical data at an effective $n$ near 109 (categorical naive Bayes, extra trees, gradient
boosting with native categorical splits, Hamming-distance $k$NN, elastic-net logistic
regression with pairwise interactions); and a \textbf{soft-voting ensemble} whose five members
were fixed \emph{before} any result was seen. Every hyperparameter and decision threshold is
selected inside the training fold; differences are tested with the Nadeau--Bengio corrected
resampled $t$-test, Holm-adjusted.

No configuration beats the plain, small-grid Random Forest, and the difference between any
original/improved pair is non-significant (Holm-adjusted $q=1.000$ for all four core
families). Two of the small-data specialists---categorical gradient boosting and the
interaction-augmented logistic regression---are directionally worse than the original Random
Forest ($q=0.144$ and $0.083$; suggestive but not significant after correction). The ensemble,
despite drawing on five tuned learners, does not exceed its best member.

This is the strongest evidence in the paper that the $\approx$0.70--0.73 ceiling is a property
of the \textbf{data} (an effective $n$ of 109 and a symptom inventory in which one item carries
the signal) rather than of insufficient modelling effort.

\subsection{Finding 5: the operating point dominates model choice}
\label{sec:audit:threshold}

At a fixed $0.5$ threshold, models varied widely in recall (0.52--0.71 across the four original
baselines) for reasons more attributable to each classifier's native score distribution than to
discriminative quality. Selecting the threshold on training folds only---the protocol used
throughout \S\ref{sec:audit:ceiling}---changed recall by as much as 0.4 for some models while
leaving AUC unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:audit:ceiling}b): every improved model's recall jumps to
0.85--0.95 once the threshold is chosen properly, at a cost in precision and specificity. For a
screening instrument---where a false negative is a missed at-risk mother and a false positive
is a follow-up conversation---the default threshold is the wrong operating point, and accuracy
at that threshold is the wrong headline metric. We recommend that screening work on this task
report decision-curve or net-benefit analysis at explicit cost ratios instead.

\subsection{Recommended protocol and corrected baselines}
\label{sec:audit:protocol}

For survey-derived mental-health datasets we recommend that evaluation (1) \textbf{group on the
unique feature pattern}, not the row; (2) \textbf{score the test fold pattern-weighted}---one
point per distinct questionnaire; (3) \textbf{tune directly on that pattern-weighted metric},
inside the training fold; and (4) report a \textbf{design effect / Kish effective $n$} alongside
the raw row count whenever the data may contain repeated or near-repeated respondents.

The corrected reference result for this dataset is:
\begin{quote}
  \textbf{ROC-AUC $= 0.730$, 95\% CI $[0.673, 0.795]$} (Random Forest, pattern-weighted,
  leakage- and weighting-controlled)---not the $\approx$\SI{99}{\percent} accuracy widely
  reported, and not improvable by extensive tuning, alternative learners, or ensembling.
\end{quote}

We recommend this dataset be treated as an \textbf{audit object rather than as training data};
work requiring a genuine structured branch should use a population survey with clinical labels
(e.g.\ PRAMS) or an EPDS-labelled cohort.

\subsection{Implication for the multimodal architecture}
\label{sec:audit:implication}

This audit changes the empirical footing of the multimodal design rather than undermining it.
The structured branch's honest ceiling---AUC $\approx 0.73$, not the $\approx 0.89$ an earlier,
row-weighted pass of this audit reported, and certainly not the $\approx$\SI{99}{\percent} in
the literature---is measured on a \emph{concurrent} severity proxy at an effective sample size
of 109. That is screening under favourable conditions, not anticipation under real ones. The
claim that a structured symptom inventory is insufficient for anticipatory PPD detection
therefore moves from a motivating assertion to a measured result, and
\S\ref{sec:audit:protocol}'s corrected baseline supplies the floor that any text or multimodal
branch must clear to justify its complexity.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:audit:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{Suicide attempt} is a self-reported severity proxy, not a clinical diagnosis,
        and is predicted from symptoms co-reported in the same instrument.
  \item The independence test in \S\ref{sec:audit:n} assumes inter-item independence and
        therefore overstates expected diversity; the assumption-free permutation test does not
        rely on it and is the claim we rest on. We do not have provenance information for the
        file and do not assert \emph{how} it was produced.
  \item Kish's design effect assumes the outcome is homogeneous within a cluster (true by
        construction here) but says nothing about \emph{why} clusters exist; it corrects for
        the consequence, not the cause.
  \item The robustness ladder in \S\ref{sec:audit:validity} tests nine items under six
        corrections; with 244--248 clusters, the most conservative tests are themselves
        low-powered, so absence of significance there is consistent with, but does not prove,
        absence of a true effect for items beyond the two (Anxious, Appetite) that fail even
        the naive test.
  \item \S\ref{sec:audit:ceiling}'s model set, while broad, is not exhaustive; we pre-specified
        the ensemble members before seeing results specifically to avoid post-hoc selection, at
        the cost of not exploring the full space of possible ensembles.
  \item The threshold analysis in \S\ref{sec:audit:threshold} optimises F1; a deployment would
        select the operating point against an explicit clinical cost ratio instead.
\end{itemize}
